Seb is a good friend and fellow regular at Civic Tech Toronto. I owe him a lot for referring me to my last job at the quantum lab.

He was the first I approached for this interview series, and we chatted about how he wants to improve civic services and democracy more broadly. Hope you all enjoy the convo!

---

{% floating title="**discovering civic tech toronto**" %}

**Khasir:**  
My first question for you, Sebastian, is: how did you first get involved with Civic Tech Toronto?

**Seb:**  
Basically, I was in undergrad doing computer science, and I was kind of annoyed. I was frustrated by the way technology was being used—in any space. And specifically, the big one for me was social media. There are all these giant applications that are trying to grab my attention, and will not allow me to do things that I want to do, which is problematic for me because I really value my time and attention.

And here I was learning all these technical skills to write software to solve any sort of problem. A lot of my friends were going into tech, and they were going to work at Facebook or Twitter. I didn’t want to do that. That didn’t seem like a good use of my time, energy, and technical skill. I was wondering if maybe there was a way to use technology for the benefit of people, rather than benefiting shareholders at the cost of democracy? So I started researching this stuff and trying to understand how to build technology for the public.

And I noticed I got really excited about this democracy aspect. How do we build better online spaces for democracy? At some point I came across the term “civic tech” and realized this is the phrase that I wanted to look for!

I googled “civic tech” and I found Civic Tech Toronto. But actually, I kind of dismissed it. I thought it wasn't quite what I was looking for. And then I was talking to my cousin and he googled “civic tech” and he told me, “No, you should really go to Civic Tech Toronto! Just try it out.” Okay, okay, I’ll try it out.

So then I went, and I was like, this is the place for me. This is exactly what I'm looking for. Here are a bunch of people who are trying to build technology not for money, but to actually benefit society in some way. And specifically, these are people who are interested in building technology for civic issues. And I think this is really where I could fit in. This aligns with my idea of building technology for democracy. So I decided I’m gonna keep coming to this place.

At the time I was in Waterloo, so I was only coming remotely, but when I moved to Toronto I was able to come in person. Then I basically came to every event after I moved. And that, I think, has been great. This community has been great. So that's how I got started.

**Khasir:**  
That’s pretty interesting. I didn’t know you googled civic tech. For me, a friend recommended me and I just stumbled into it.

**Seb:**  
I did need that push—my cousin did have to come in and be like, “You should try this thing again.” \[laughs] So friend recommendations definitely help.

**Khasir:**  
That leads to my next question, which is: to you, what does *civic tech* mean? As opposed to Civic Tech Toronto?

**Seb:**   
So, civic technology—it's interesting because I run [Civic Tech 101](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1tC9O3EpLjW-UDU08uVliMu8riBltTc4M4jOadJaV6fU/edit?usp=drive_link) these days, and so this is something that we keep talking about. It's evolved as I've attended Civic Tech Toronto, and especially as I've done Civic Tech 101. But I think that civic tech, the essence, at least within this community, seems to be building technology for public good.

And I found this distinction early on, that it doesn't mean you're building something for government. There's a distinction between *government tech*, which improves the efficiency and ability for the public service to deliver to the public, and *civic tech*, which is more of building technology directly for residents or citizens. At least, I think that’s how this community defines it.

For me personally, I really value the democracy aspect. [Ilya](/interviews/TODO), who's also a member of Civic Tech Toronto, has a really good way of saying this: we're building democratic infrastructure. He works on the [Civic Dashboard](https://civicdashboard.ca/) project, I was working on [Polis](https://pol.is/), and all of these things are just platforms or tools to improve the way that we interface with democracy.

And that's the thing that I get excited about. So that's how I define it. Which is maybe more specific than what all of Civic Tech Toronto as a community defines *civic tech*.

**Khasir:**  
I find that everyone has their own definition of *civic tech*, because it’s such a broad concept that people latch onto one thing or another. And that thing is, for them, civic tech.

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**understanding systems**" %}

I remember you mentioned at one point that you didn’t really understand how the Canadian government system works, and it’s only recently that you re-learned how that works. Could you talk about that?

**Seb:**  
I think this is a problem just with civic tech people in general—I include myself in this. We want to solve problems. And we don't spend the time to understand what those problems are before we start solving them. And I got caught in the same thing: “We're going to build a platform that'll benefit society tomorrow. And we're not going to spend the time to understand the details of how all this stuff works.”

**Khasir:**  
Build fast.

**Seb:**    
Right. Ideally, build fast and *not* break things. Build fast and improve things. But I would say, more so, what ends up happening is you build fast and then don't accomplish your goals. It fizzles.

I think to build something that actually is going to be beneficial for people, you have to start with who that you're building for. For me, even when I was focusing on Polis, I was trying to build a tool that maybe city councilors would use. They would have this ability to do surveys with residents within their community and gather a broad consensus of what that community wants. And then I realized somewhere along the line that I don't know the process. Or how city council works. That's not good. \[laughs]

If I'm gonna be delivering something to city council, I’m gonna need to know what they do, right? And then I was thinking more about it, and I realized I don't know how we make decisions in democracy in the first place! Okay, I gotta take a step back here \[laughs] and figure out what's going on.

The best way I learn things is, I watch YouTube videos. I'll eat my lunch and I'll watch a YouTube video of some sort of educational content. And so, I found a [playlist from Crash Course](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL8dPuuaLjXtOfse2ncvffeelTrqvhrz8H) by Hank and John Green on YouTube about American democracy. Very American-centric, but for the most part, democracies follow the same model. So I learned about the three different branches of government:

1. Judicial,
2. Legislative, and 
3. Executive.

Those three all work together, and the way that I picture it in my head is this triangle, and they're all keeping each other in check, preventing anybody from getting too much power.

And this is this exciting thing: you start looking at how the structure works, why democracy is stable, and why it doesn't devolve into anarchy, or more likely, authoritarianism. It's because you have these different entities that are all competing and protecting their own interests and checking each other.

And so I learned about that, and I thought, this is an interesting system. I love systems. I love power systems and understanding how they work. This got me more excited about democracy. Now that I have that basis, I will be able to create something that will actually benefit the systems that we have right now.

> I think that the mirror \[tech companies] are holding up is warped. Warped in such a way that we see the worst parts of humanity.

{% floating title="**citizen assemblies as democracy**" %}

**Khasir:**  
So you mentioned Polis[^1]—what drew you to work on Polis, and where do you hope to take it?

**Seb:**  
Polis came about from a bunch of different spots. Basically, I kept listening to podcasts, I kept talking to people, and Polis kept coming up as a tool to scale these things called *[citizens assemblies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens'_assembly)*.

Briefly, citizens assemblies are basically what I think the legislative branch should be (I won't get into that), where you randomly select individuals from a community such that they mirror the demographics of that community. You get them into a room and you have a discussion. Usually, there's some sort of educational component and afterwards the deliberation component, where the citizens actually tell you what they want. Then they come up with policy recommendations that are given to the public service or politician to be implemented.

This idea is really fascinating to me because—seeing the way that the internet was evolving into this chaotic screaming match of unproductivity and the polarization that we're seeing all around the world— it was encouraging to see you can actually get people into a room and have a discussion. And then I thought, if we can get 40 people into a room, that's good. But can you scale that? Not really, right? You have to do some sort of online thing.

There was a [talk at Civic Tech Toronto from Mass LBP](https://civictech.ca/events/376/). They’re an organization that runs citizens assemblies across Canada. I actually got to ask them a question:

How do you scale this?
{: .large style="text-align: center"}

And they said:

Polis.
{: .large style="text-align: center"}

I had already heard about it from this podcast called [Your Undivided Attention](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast) produced by the Center for Humane Technology. They interviewed Audrey Tang from Taiwan, and she says in it that Polis is the thing that they use in Taiwan. So there's multiple different avenues of people saying Polis is the way to have these conversations at scale in a collaborative manner. Which is great because this is the online space that I'm looking for, that is much better and bolsters democracy, compared to your Facebooks, your Twitters, your Instagrams. Ideally we would see more of that in the world today.

**Khasir:**  
I think a lot of social media these days is very driven on conflict and pure engagement, as opposed to really engaging with the values of: why did we invent these in the first place? To allow people to have a voice on the ground, right? That's democracy.

I think it's great that Polis, instead of dividing people, goes back in the direction of bringing people together and having a fruitful discussion.

**Seb:**  
This, I think, is critical here. Tech companies have been known to say, “Oh, we're just holding up a mirror to humanity. That’s what we're doing when we create these platforms.” I disagree. I think that the mirror that they're holding up is warped. Warped in such a way that we see the worst parts of humanity.

It's obvious because the algorithm that they use is specifically designed to enrage us! [Ragebait is the term of 2025](https://corp.oup.com/news/the-oxford-word-of-the-year-2025-is-rage-bait/), right? I think it's possible to create an environment online that promotes the best natures of humanity. But, of course, the money's not there. So as a result, you end up with this dystopian nightmare of a system.

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**work life**" %}

**Khasir:**  
Switching topics a bit, I recently switched jobs. Before that, I was working at the Quantum Photonics Lab, which you referred me to. I was wondering how you ended up working at such a place in the first place? ‘Cause you’ve mentioned to me that you were referred there by someone else.

How did you get in a position to be working there, at the Quantum Photonics Lab? You have a degree in physics, right?

**Seb:**  
Not quite.

**Khasir:**  
Not quite, okay. What is it?

**Seb:**  
I have half a degree in physics, which means I don't have a degree. \[laughs] I did two years of physics and I was trying to do a computer science minor. Then I realized that I enjoy computer science more than physics, so I switched. I was going to do a computer science major and a physics minor, and then the physics minor disappeared. So I still have some of that physics background, which I think they were excited about when I did the interview: “Great, you have some physics and you have the computer science so you would be a good person to hire.”

The person who was previously in that role was leaving for Toronto. He was the friend of my friend from karate.

**Khasir:**  
Whoa.

**Seb:**  
Yeah I know, right? It's a bit of a thing. I used to do karate at the University of Waterloo. I met my friend Ben there, and then he referred me to his roommate. Since this friend was leaving to go to Toronto to start a new job, he knew I was looking for a position and messaged me and said, “Hey, would you want this position?” I read the description and I thought, whoa, this sounds like a really cool job. So I applied with a reference, and then they asked me for an interview. We did the interview. And they hired me, I think within a week or two? It was a part-time position. So it wasn't like they were going to hire me full time. So then I worked with them on their software stuff. And because it was part time, it allowed me to work on the civic tech stuff that I wanted to work on.

Having that income stream, so then I can focus on other things that I want to do is quite useful, in terms of being able to get the dreams that you want to get done. Hard to get paid to do the things you want to do. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
Yeah, civic tech does not garner a lot of money here in Canada. I'm very glad you referred me there. It was a very good stop gap. It was very different from civic tech, very different from anything I've done before. But it was also soul draining. \[laughs] I'm glad you referred me, but I would not go back.

**Seb:**  
Yeah, I think that lab has some things to work out in terms of maintaining people and retaining people. They suffer from, I would say, too much stuff being done by upper management; they need to de-scope a little bit. I mean, we can't really do much about it, but it's just from what I observed. I never got a chance to talk to anybody there about that. Also, being fully remote, it’s hard to do that.

> The amount of red tape in the government is insane. The reason we can't deliver on many things is because government is too risk averse. I would encourage the public to demand better services. And be okay with some risk.
> {: .large}

I think some of these university labs really focus in on certain things that aren't as important as they might seem to be. Analyzing every line of code has some value, definitely, to create a useful, stable product. But at the cost of the user interface? It took me almost a month to run that code because there were three different aspects to run it, all in different terminals and then I had to connect them all together. So, understandable that it was soul draining at times.

**Khasir:**  
For sure. But now you're on to bigger and better things! Like your current job—you work at the City of Toronto. Can you remind me again, which department it is?

**Seb:**  
Currently, I work at [Toronto Public Health](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/city-administration/staff-directory-divisions-and-customer-service/toronto-public-health/). That was my goal, to get into government. I saw that there were opportunities there to improve the way that government works. It's no secret that government's not very good at technology. I think it's good that I got into the team because my manager is excited to build cool things! He's always about bringing the innovation. I think he sees that I'm excited to build good things and deliver for—what we call at the city—the businesses. In normal speak, it's the clinics that we support.

For example, we support some of the sexual health clinics in the city. The not–safe injection sites anymore because [those have changed](https://theconversation.com/ontario-is-closing-its-supervised-consumption-sites-calling-them-a-failure-so-what-counts-as-success-279288). Now, they're called HRTS, which are the harm reduction sites. We also support the tuberculosis electronic medical record system that was actually built in-house, surprisingly, which is amazing. And the cold chain information system, which monitors refrigerators that hold publicly-funded vaccines. And the cold chain system is actually the one that my boss built back in 2012. We're maintaining that today.

I'm all for this. I'm all for government building the stuff internally so that we can actually see the data and we can actually see the way that the data is processed. When you work with vendors, you don't know what you're going to get. Some vendors are amazing, and they produce really good products. Some vendors … I don't even know what they're doing. They don't even know what they're doing.

**Khasir:**  
\[laughs]

{% floating title="**government efficiency**" %}

**Seb:**  
I won't name names for this. But in my experience, it's quite a broad range. So if we can own the code, then we can make it a good product, no matter what. As long as the people in the team want to do that and put the effort in. But if you have people who want to put the effort in and they can't because it's a vendor product (stupid), you can't do anything.

**Khasir:**  
I think people tend to forget that there's a lot of value in having knowledge and expertise in-house, as opposed to [outsourcing or doing an RFP](https://web.archive.org/web/20151106150138/http://www.world-psi.org/sites/default/files/documents/research/rapport_eng_56pages_a4_lr_0.pdf) where some [third-party contractor](https://cupe.ca/privatization-issue) will do it. ‘Cause then, you build that knowledge, that expertise. And if you need to change something, you can do it. As opposed to having to go through this process of needing to change the contract to support a new feature.

**Seb:**  
Not to mention the procurement process is very lengthy. Six months is the minimum. And this is something that I'm going to mention here, because I think it's really important to understand: the amount of red tape in the government is insane. The reason we can't deliver on many things is because government is too risk averse. I would encourage the public to demand better services. And be okay with some risk. I'm not saying, be super risky, and do stupid things. All I'm saying is, we've gone too far in the other direction. We're too constraining to the point where it'll take two or three days to do the technical aspect, and a month to do the paperwork. And I don't think that's acceptable.

I want to deliver for the public. But I can't. I think it'd be really, really cool to see an organization be all about cutting red tape in government. Because government should be able to deliver and unfortunately, on both sides of the aisle, we've crippled it. It's unable to deliver the services at the level at which the private sector can. I think we should meet that standard. I don't think we should try to strive for anything less.

**Khasir:**  
Why do you think we've reached this point of so much red tape that it takes 10 times as long to get through anything?

**Seb:**  
From the way that I understand it, and this is largely based off the book [Abundance by Ezra Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_\(Klein_and_Thompson_book\)), there were a few laws that were passed around environmental protection which made it really easy to sue government—this is US-centric, so I apologize. Government reacted to this and said, “Okay, we need to be careful about what we do, so we don't get sued.”

That in combination with—this is from talking to people at the city who've been there for a while—there have been a few scandals where public service employees have funnelled money into their own accounts. Embezzlement and whatnot, and there was an overreaction to it. We gotta add more checks and more balances to this process to make sure that no money is funnelled anywhere.

I think that, yeah, we need checks and balances. But we need to find a balance between a process that prevents data leaks (I think we're better at that than the private sector, which leaks data all the freaking time), while also being able to deliver. We gotta take back some of these laws. Making it a little harder to sue government, I think, would be beneficial. And not overreacting to certain situations where, yeah, someone embezzled some money, but tweak it in such a way that it works, that it improves things.

And there's another thing here. I read this book last year, [Recoding America](https://www.recodingamerica.us/), where the author argues that there are times when actually repealing a restriction is beneficial to service delivery. She gives the example of, during COVID, there are people trying to get their stimulus cheques from the government. But they were caught up in this process. Everyone was asking for this, and there were just a bunch of tickets in the system. So what the government of California did was say, “Okay, what we're going to do is just hire more people.” Which makes sense, right? To hire more people, that would then allow them to get through all the tickets and solve all the issues. But it turns out that when you hire people, you have to train them, and the training was done by the people who were pros and experts in the system, so they were taken off of doing the tickets. And instead, they had to help train these new people.

Meanwhile, some of these call centers had to help residents who didn't speak English or speak a language that the public service employees knew. So what they had to do is get a translator, but of course, there's this giant process to get a translator. What would have actually helped is taking out the process of getting a translator from this chaotic mass of a situation, because then it would be much faster to help that person and deliver the stimulus cheques they needed. So this is an example of repealing some red tape that would then allow the public service to deliver for the public.

**Khasir:**  
Reducing red tape was a big topic, especially around the election, and also around US politics as well. That was a big sticking point with Trump. So, how do we make sure we don't go too far in the other direction of overcorrecting again and getting rid of everything?

**Seb:**  
I think that's the thing. We have to strike a balance. We have to do an analysis, and figure out, where can we cut? I think it was back in the 90s, the Clinton administration actually cut the public service. It’s weird that I'm saying this, but I think this is beneficial because it finds efficiencies. They did it in a smart way. They did it in a way where they looked through the processes that currently existed, and they said, “Okay, what do we need and what don't we need? Oh, we don't need these processes? Great, let's get rid of them. Then we can condense this team of 20 people into maybe 5, and still get the work done. Maybe the 15 people can be better utilized in other parts of the government.”

I'm not saying totally get rid of them. I'm just saying we should be hiring people to do the things that benefit the public most. Specifically to answer your question, look at the processes that currently exist, and cut out the stuff that you don't need. But that takes time. And I think [DOGE](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Department-of-Government-Efficiency-United-States) was not that.

**Khasir:**  
\[laughs] No.

**Seb:**  
No, that was, “let's just cut everything,” which backfired on them because they had to rehire everybody that they fired for more money. If anything, now it costs the government money to do all those cuts, which then ended up in more bloat.

**Khasir:**  
To me, that gets to the heart of civic tech. All we really want to do is bring the benefits of technology to civic processes, and allow them to be more efficient, more nimble where necessary, or create solutions that work for these people. And bringing government into the 21st century, where sometimes it's lacking.

I just want to close off this interview with one question I’m asking everyone, which is: what does Civic Tech Toronto mean to you, specifically?

**Seb:**  
That's a big question.

So as I mentioned, I did karate at the University of Waterloo, and that was my community there. I met a lot of friends there. I had a great time there. Then I left Waterloo. I came to Toronto. Civic Tech Toronto is now my new community.

I’ve made almost all the friends that I hang out with currently from Civic Tech Toronto. That is, I think, one of the big reasons that keeps me coming back. Having that community, getting excited about the same things, and building technology with that. Building stuff because we all know we all care, and we all want to deliver. I think it's a lot easier to build things with other people. Building something on your own is a lot more difficult. So, as soon as you have other people that you are also excited to hang around, you're going to create cool stuff.
