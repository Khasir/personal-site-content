Gabe is one of the co-founders of Civic Tech Toronto. Despite his stony exterior, he conceals a kindness and thoughtfulness that is reflected in the community he has helped to create. In the basement of 1RG on a sunny day in mid-October, I asked Gabe some questions about CTTO and how it has thrived for so long.

---

> \[S]eeing civic tech projects and civic tech teams work, it really reaffirms how the technology is the easy part.
> {: .large}

**Gabe:**  
To help me make my answers as useful as possible, what is it about the essence of civic tech that you want to capture, or what inspired you to do this?

**Khasir:**  
I've phrased it as, I want to capture a time capsule of Civic Tech Toronto. I want people to know what it was like to be part of Civic Tech Toronto in 2026, capture the culture, the zeitgeist, the people—

**Gabe:**  
Why?

**Khasir:**  
You can think of this as being part of my role as Archivist. But also, I listen to a lot of podcasts, and I think podcasts are a very approachable way for people to feel part of a community without actually being part of that community. And I think Civic Tech Toronto is a great community. I think it's quite unique and a lot of the people I talk to also think it's quite unique. I just care about it a lot, and I want to remember it, you know?

**Gabe:**  
Love it. Thanks!

{% floating title="**looking back on civic tech toronto**" %}

**Khasir:**  
\[*laughs*] Moving on to you!

You are one of the co-founders of Civic Tech Toronto. I know you don't like the spotlight, but—as I already said—I think Civic Tech Toronto is quite a special community. How did you, yourself, decide that you wanted to start a community centred on civic tech? Given that especially 10 years ago, and even now, most people don't even know what civic tech is?

**Gabe:**  
When I came to learn of civic tech, which would have been probably around 2013 or 2014, a bunch of stuff just came into perspective. And I was like, oh, this is the thing that I've been looking for a long time! So I learned a bit about the work that was going on in different places and had the chance to take a trip to Chicago to go visit a civic tech event that was happening there. And came back really inspired by, not only the work that was happening, but also the values behind the work and the way that they were organizing.

It just really resonated with me. I had done some work around what I later came to know as *civic tech* or *community tech*. And so I just knew that this was important. This resonated for me a lot and I hoped that it would resonate for others. And it was really me being aware of work that was happening, community groups that were organizing around civic tech in the US and in Europe, and I was just jealous. I thought, hey, why don’t we have this here? Okay, well, someone's got to do it. So, I talked about it with folks for a while and we were like, okay, we just need to make the commitment to do it and start. Got some great advice from folks in Chicago and elsewhere, and did it.

**Khasir:**  
You've been a part of Civic Tech Toronto for a long time. You mentioned to me at one point that you haven't been here the whole time, but you've been here for most of the time. Have you noticed things change in your time that you've been involved? Would you say that there's different vibes in different years?

**Gabe:**  
Some things have changed over time and some things haven't. Which feels right! The biggest change happened while I wasn't involved. In March 2020, the organizing team at the time needed to make a decision about whether to continue doing hacknights[^1] or not. I think possibly the most important decision in Civic Tech Toronto’s history was made, which was to continue doing hacknights and just move them onto Zoom. 

That means that Civic Tech Toronto didn't miss a week through the pandemic. A pandemic that essentially wiped out a lot of community organizing, community meeting. But Civic Tech Toronto is one of the few groups that kept going. And not just kept going, but actually was strengthened in a lot of ways because it was a lifeline, so to speak, for folks who sought out that kind of community through those days. So, I think the move to online and then the subsequent move to hybrid were the most visible changes.

I think things that haven't changed are the format, mostly. That remains still very familiar to how it started. The values. Though, certainly, the better understanding we have about the role of Big Tech and the impact of social media on people has certainly really helped refine the approach that we take as a community and how we articulate the values.

What's changed in the events? Not so much. What’s changed on the Slack? Also not so much. I think that one of the things that makes Civic Tech Toronto unique, like you say, and it's a little bit hard for people to wrap their heads around, is that: one of the things that changes is all of the organizers. That if you look at who's organizing with Civic Tech Toronto, and then you fast-forward two years, and then look at who's organizing Civic Tech Toronto, it's pretty much none of the same people. That there's an almost complete leadership turnover every two or three years. And that can be a bit disorienting because it's like, who's in charge? But actually, we have a really good system in place for figuring that out. And it means that this thing that is *kind of a lot of work* doesn't burn out any individual person. And actually gets stronger and evolves with the interests and new energy of new folks that come to it.

**Khasir:**  
In my time at Civic Tech Toronto, I have seen a lot of faces. There are regulars that do come here and there are a lot of overlaps, but it has changed in terms of the people. And yet, would you say the culture has changed? Or would you say that that has stayed relatively stable? Because I, for one, find the culture to be very open, very trusting, and unlike most other tech groups in Toronto.

How do you think that culture came to be? Was that something you cultivated? Was that something that just emerged naturally?

**Gabe:**  
Yeah, I do think that there has been stability of the culture. Not to say that it's frozen in time or anything, but I think that there's a lot of consistency to it. I also think that, as someone has been around for a while, I'm primed to see what's similar over time, whereas other folks might focus on what's changed over time, and I think that both of those are valid.

I used to go to a lot of tech events, and I don't anymore, so I can't really speak to how our events are similar or different to other tech events these days. I do know that, back when I was going to a lot of tech events, I found a lot of them really gross, in a way that was maybe a little bit difficult for me to articulate. But I also went to a lot of community events that I found really warm and welcoming.

I think that a lot of what’s baked into the format and the governance of Civic Tech Toronto is very intentional to reflect values and to create a welcoming space that prioritizes the things that are important to us, to the group of people who find this community compelling. The things that come to mind are:

- The structure of breakout groups where there's no vetting process or anything. Anyone who wants to run a breakout group can just put up their hand and run a breakout group.
- The way in which we recruit volunteers, which is just by asking people to volunteer. And amazingly, that works out.

**Khasir:**  
It worked for me!

**Gabe:**    
It seems like it shouldn't work, but it actually really works.

And then things like:

- Inviting people to introduce themselves. A whole room of people and a whole online room of people are invited to introduce themselves at the beginning of every event.

It sounds bonkers and sometimes it doesn't feel like a great use of time, but it's actually empowering. Empowering is not the right word. My next-best word is self-affirming, in a way. Where each person in the room is invited to speak, use their voice out loud, in a room where everyone else is listening. I think there's something about that which is implicitly saying, I am here. In a way that we often feel quite anonymous in a group of people, and that we tentatively walk in, find a seat at the back, sit down, listen, and then maybe duck out early. Where, were we even really there? Was that an experience? It doesn't feel like community, but that everyone is listening to you, even if it's just for 10 seconds. There's something quite affirming about that.

**Khasir:**  
I think so. I never really thought of it that way, but I do find that that is one of the things that distinguishes Civic Tech Toronto. We spend the first 10–20 minutes getting everyone to say their names, pronouns, and what they're grateful for. Most other communities don't do that.

**Gabe:**  
No, some people hate it. And like I said, it doesn't always feel like an awesome use of time, but I think it aligns really closely with our values. And I think that it lends itself to greater participation, and invites people to lean in more than they would otherwise.

{% floating title="**comparisons to code for canada**" %}

**Khasir:**  
You are also co-founder of [Code for Canada](https://codefor.ca/). Could you describe that civic tech organization, and how that relates to Civic Tech Toronto?

**Gabe:**  
Code for Canada is a non-profit that was started in 2017. I was the co-founder and executive director of it. I'd say its intent—I don't know if they still phrase it this way now—was to help connect innovators in government with the civic tech community, in order to help advance the good work and the good services that government delivers. And acknowledge that often government struggles to do great work with technology and to adopt modern tech practice, because it's genuinely difficult to adapt that practice to the government context. Our intent with standing that up was to help build some of those connections between the civic tech community and folks in government who would like to work with them.

**Khasir:**  
One thing you mentioned is that it’s a non-profit. That’s actually different than Civic Tech Toronto, which is not a non-profit. It’s also not a corporation, to be clear. What went into deciding that Code for Canada should be a non-profit versus Civic Tech Toronto just staying as it is, which is just a community organization?

**Gabe:**  
They are organizations that have certainly adjacent mandates, purpose and community, but operate with very different models. Civic Tech Toronto is about building community, conversation, and collaboration around civic tech. Whereas Code for Canada is more concrete in the sense that there are people who are getting paid, and partnerships with specific deliverables that are happening between government and the civic tech community. There's a lot more risk, liability and resources that's involved with those kinds of formal relationships that can only be done with an incorporated group, a board of directors, staff and all that stuff. So they operate at different scales, but hopefully in a way that's complimentary.

I could speak to how I think of Civic Tech Toronto's relationship to money and resources, which is the total opposite approach.

**Khasir:**  
Yeah, I think that would be interesting. 

**Gabe:**  
I probably have stronger views about this than some people, but with Civic Tech Toronto—this may have evolved over time—it was intended, designed to be a thing that could happen without money. Yeah, we need space, and it's helpful to be able to have samosas or pizza or something, but it was really designed as a thing that could still happen if there was no money. Which is, I think, the best way to design a thing like that. And if you design something that doesn't need money, then actually, having money becomes a liability in a way, right? Someone needs to hold it. You need to protect it. How do you get it? You need to manage how you spend it. This money is essentially a community resource, and so what's the right way to manage or steward that?

There's all kinds of hairy questions that come out of that, which are often not the fun questions to answer. I think what’s an important value in the way that we organize Civic Tech Toronto is that, these are volunteer things, and volunteer stuff should prioritize stuff that's fun. Because it becomes a risk—the more not-fun jobs there are, the more fragile the thing will be, because you may not always be able to find people who do that not-fun stuff. So let's try to design out the not-fun stuff. Money is one of those not-fun things.

So we've just tried to be as lean as possible with having money. We don't go especially out of our way to try to get money. We try to make sure that for the money that we have, we’re able to stretch it as far as possible without any of the management of it being onerous.

**Khasir:**  
Honestly, I think this is also a contributing factor to why the culture has focused on openness and sharing. There is no financial incentive for people to participate, so it doesn't attract the kind of people that would go after money. It creates a space for everyone else who doesn't care as much about that, or is not in a position to care about that.

**Gabe:**  
Yeah, totally. I really love that way of framing it. I think that's such a strong point. I think that civic technologists and civic tech projects tend to be enormously resourceful. Certainly, sometimes you do need resources for something, but these groups and these folks, when they're really mission-driven, when they really care about the work that they're doing, are able to leverage relationships and dig through the couch cushions and find what they need in order to get stuff done, in a way that is far more efficient than trying to do that centrally.

If we offered free AWS credits or something like that, I just think that that would end up being a resource that’s not used super effectively. Whereas if the groups are seeking those things out on their own, which they totally can, it just sort of works out much better. There's a culture of sharing resources, sharing opportunities, sharing network, that helps lift us all hopefully.

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**gabe’s involvement in civic tech projects**" %}

**Khasir:**  
You've been involved in a few civic tech projects as part of Civic Tech Toronto: [Tabs Toronto](http://pwd.ca/tabs), [Toronto Bids](https://github.com/CivicTechTO/toronto-bids/), and most recently [tRacket](https://tracket.info/). Could you briefly describe each of those and how you were involved in them?

**Gabe:**  
Tabs Toronto is a project that I started probably in 2021 or 2022. But it was actually just a rebuild of someone else's project. It was possibly one of the first civic tech projects in Toronto that I was aware of and was from at least 10 years prior to that. It came out of a Random Hacks of Kindness hackathon very early on that I didn't participate in. Some folks who were affiliated with the [Toronto Public Space Committee](https://www.publicspace.ca/) created Toronto Bids as a tool for tracking City of Toronto council agendas, in order to make it easier for folks to track items or topics that they cared about that were coming up. City of Toronto agendas are posted seven days in advance, and so this is just a little crawler that takes a look at those and just sends you an email if a topic that you've signed up for notifications on is coming up, to give you a heads up so that you can go and [depute](https://civicdashboard.ca/wiki/Deputations.html) if you want to.

There were some folks who stood that up many, many years ago. I found that super inspiring and also super useful. I know that many others did as well, and at some point, it went offline. \[*laughs*] As happens, after a decade with the civic tech projects, commonly.

I remember getting a call from someone who was doing advocacy work at an environmental non-profit. They said, I really miss Tabs, that project. It helped me and a lot of our sister organizations to keep track of what's going on, and having to do that manually now means that I can't go on vacation. Or if I go on vacation, I'm afraid that we're going to miss something that's happening at Council. So I was like, oh yeah, wow. It really brought to me that both the people doing real advocacy work were depending on these tools and the impact of those tools not being available.

I had a little bit of time around then, so I rebuilt it with the permission of the original creators and relaunched it. So it's a very slight project, but a useful one. That was Tabs on Toronto.

Toronto Bids is a project that's still very much a work in progress. In a way, it's sort of like Tabs on Toronto, but for procurement. It keeps an eye on City of Toronto bids—goods and services that they are looking to buy that are posted online—by grabbing the bid documents associated with that and archiving them, in order to make them available. Because those documents are often only available for a small window of time, but they can offer an enormous amount of value to researchers and to small businesses interested in knowing more about what and when and how the City of Toronto buys.

And tRacket is an open hardware project, where our team designed a simple, low-cost sound-level monitor. We were inspired by a group called [No More Noise Toronto](https://nomorenoise.ca/) that was doing advocacy around awareness of the public health impacts of noise in the city. One of the things that we learned about the issue, is that there's actually very little data about noise levels overall. There is equipment that you can buy or rent that you can install in order to monitor noise levels, but that equipment is incredibly expensive and generally designed for short-term installation. Like they'll do a study where they install it for a week. So then you have a week of data about noise levels, but what you might want to see is, how have noise levels changed over time? over months? over years? Or what are the patterns based on day of the week? by weather? time of year?

There wasn't really a clear solution for doing that. And certainly not a solution that would be affordable for regular residents. So we designed a cheap device that will listen for noise levels, monitor decibel levels, which is just a simple number, and then save that to a website where you can go, and you can visualize the data over time at any location.

We've been working on that project for a few years. It's an evening and weekend project for us. But we're really proud of what we've built, and it very slowly continues to grow.

**Khasir:**  
I remember you were assembling them recently. Is that something you're still doing?

**Gabe:**  
\[*laughs*] Yeah. For this summer, we did a pilot project with No More Noise. A collaboration with them where No More Noise were the folks who were doing the promo and essentially selling these kits to folks who wanted to install them. We were supplying the devices and doing the shipping and doing the support, as a trial to just see—we did almost 50 devices out in the wild now, that we have in four different time zones on two continents at the moment.

**Khasir:**  
Wow.

**Gabe:**  
That's a big step up for us. So far it's gone quite well. We need to do some debriefs and retros on this pilot still, but we've certainly learned a lot.

**Khasir:**  
It sounds like it's going well then. It's funny, because there's no breakout for this at Civic Tech Toronto, I have no idea what's going on with tRacket. Yet it sounds like you're very successful. The pilot’s going well with No More Noise, and you've deployed a lot of these devices.

**Gabe:**  
I think that there's an interesting thing there that we still are learning about collectively which is, when is it appropriate for civic tech projects to be meeting actively in breakouts at meetups? and when to pause on that? and when to graduate from that into meeting elsewhere? and when to focus on getting the thing done? It's going to vary a lot from project to project.

But yeah, I think that speaks to part of the importance of the [project archive](https://civictech.ca/projects/) on the Civic Tech Toronto website. But also keeping in touch. We still have a [noise meter Slack channel](https://civictechto.slack.com/archives/C05LHL4L8MD) that we post to. Not very often, but we certainly monitor. At some point, would love to come back and maybe do a presentation update at a meetup on how things are going with tRacket.

**Khasir:**  
Who else is involved? Is it a large group?

**Gabe:**  
There are four main contributors. We were all Toronto-based initially. But now Clyne, who does the hardware, has moved back to Maine. Dan, who built the dashboard, has moved to Europe. And Mitch, who runs the API and the database, splits his time between Toronto and Buffalo. We all have a lot of stuff going on and have had some life changes, but we're all still really committed to the project, which I'm so grateful for.

**Khasir:**  
That's great. You have a remote team now. You're international. \[*laughs*]

**Gabe:**  
Yeah, that’s another reason why we aren't doing highlights a lot. \[*laughs*] Though Mitch is there—not all the time, but often—working on other projects.

**Khasir:**  
With Toronto Bids, how were you involved with that? What did you do?

**Gabe:**  
There was like a really wonderful, long period of time where we were meeting regularly at hacknights to figure out what this should be. Creating a bunch of prototypes and trying a bunch of different things. I think that really helped us sharpen what it needed to be. At this stage now, there's really two of us working on it: me and [Alex Olson](/notes/coming-soon). It currently has no functional frontend. The focus of the work lately has been on this scraper and archive part of it.

At some point, we'll work on the frontend and rebuild that piece. But in the meantime we're continuing to collect data, which I feel like is the important piece right for that. The frontend will make it available to people, but in the meantime, we're collecting data that will make it more valuable once we do launch it.

**Khasir:**  
In terms of Tabs Toronto, it sounds a lot like what [Civic Dashboard](https://civicdashboard.ca/) aims to do today. I don't know Tabs Toronto very well, but do you see if there is overlap? Do you see Civic Dashboard as replacing it?

**Gabe:**  
Oh yeah, totally. I do for sure. Thankfully, Civic Dashboard, and other projects as well, do what Tabs does and more. I would love to sunset Tabs Toronto at some point, if it feels that what we do is entirely covered by someone else, and there’s a confidence that that will continue to be available for folks. It's not like we have a huge userbase with Tabs Toronto, but that's something that's been on the back of my mind. I haven't pursued it actively, but I would love to someday transition Tabs users over to Civic Dashboard or something else. Totally.

{% floating title="**what makes a project successful**" %}

**Khasir:**  
You've seen a lot of projects come and go. There are a lot of presentations that come and go. They have their apps that they work on, and then who knows what happens to them in a few years. What do you think are some common elements in terms of what makes a project successful?

**Gabe:**  
That's a really tough one to answer because projects take so many different forms. It depends on the objectives. If we're assuming that we mean a project that meets at hacknights regularly for a long time and brings in new volunteers and produces some exciting outputs, it takes:

- A compelling story. A narrative.
- Representing something inspiring, something that people see as worthwhile.
- Being both ambitious and realistic at the same time.
- Having both
  - a civic relevance, where it solves a problem for people,
  - and that it represents a compelling opportunity to build and/or learn for those who would contribute to it. People who see the chance to use skills that they have in a meaningful way, or people who see a chance to have a context for developing or practicing new skills.

It takes some level of:

- volunteer management,
- logistics,
- communication,
- having people feel like what they're contributing to is being managed well, coordinated well.

In most cases, it takes a lot of effort. A surprisingly large amount of effort needs to go into onboarding new folks.

It probably takes having fun. I mean, that's one of those things that really helps a lot. That people are bringing a positive, playful attitude to it where they're realistic about what can be expected of volunteers, without losing the ambition.

That's just a mishmash of things. But I think all of those are important.

**Khasir:**  
I think so, too. The storytelling, for example, is underestimated especially by the tech part of civic tech. That's not something that I think comes naturally to a lot of people who just want to make things and build things. I think that's important.

**Gabe:**  
Yeah, I totally agree with that. If anything, seeing civic tech projects and civic tech teams work, it really reaffirms how the technology is the easy part.

{% floating title="**the future of civic tech toronto**" %}

**Khasir:**  
For sure. So, what are your hopes for the future of Civic Tech Toronto? What do you hope that it does in the future, or it continues to do?

**Gabe:**  
Maybe this is where this might seem unambitious for me, but I'd like to think my hopes and expectations for Civic Tech Toronto are pretty modest, because to set overly ambitious expectations is unrealistic in a volunteer context.

The nice thing about Civic Tech Toronto is that it can scale to the ambition of the folks who are involved. For example, we ran a [really giant conference](https://civic-spark.com/) last year. We've [run](https://civictech.ca/announcements/codeacross-toronto-community-collaboration/) [really](https://civictech.ca/announcements/codeacross-toronto-2017-recap/) [big](https://civictech.ca/announcements/codeacross-toronto-2018/) [hackathons](https://civictech.ca/announcements/codeacross-toronto-2019/) in the past. We can only do those when there's a surplus of energy and ambition on the part of the team, which isn't all the time.

Mostly, I just hope that it keeps doing what it does. Which is to be a container, or a meeting place for people to find each other to have important, meaningful, interesting, fun conversations about technology and data and design, and what the potential of those things are, and also where we need to be very cautious in the way that we implement those things. Be a place for people to find each other.

What's great about not taking a lot of money, is it means you don't have a lot of accountability, which means that we don't need to do impact measurement or evaluation or anything like that. We can just do the thing that's fun, that's interesting, that’s compelling for us.

People keep coming, which demonstrates that others find it meaningful too. I know that lots of great things have come from it, in terms of projects, in terms of impacts of those projects, but more importantly, in the way that individuals have learned a lot about technology, but more importantly, civics, government, collaboration, community organizing, advocacy, politics even.

Early on, we had this crisis of purpose, so [we did a theory of change exercise](https://medium.com/@liamilito/clarifying-impact-lessons-learned-at-civic-tech-toronto-d3d3b85318d7), which was super eye-opening for me. I think back to that a lot—what is it that we're trying to do here? It's very much about helping equip people to be great citizens. Great civic actors. I think we're doing that. We have some anecdotal stories to share about that happening. We don't have so much data or evidence about it, but we know that it's happening.

It may not be perfect, but I think that it's surprisingly effective, and has been reasonably sustainable so far.

**Khasir:**  
Kudos.

**Gabe:**  
Likewise.

**Khasir:**  
\[*laughs*] Thanks.

{% floating title="**the meaning of civic tech toronto**" %}

I just want to end this interview with the same question I'm asking to everyone, which is: what does Civic Tech Toronto mean to you?

**Gabe:**  
I think that may have changed a little bit over time. Or maybe it ebbs and flows or something, but sometimes I think of it as, especially as I am getting older. I think of it as a space that I really wish had existed when I was earlier in my career or earlier in my education. These are the conversations that I wanted to have, with the people I wanted to have it with, that didn't exist at the time.

I really hope that it represents a different kind of vision for what good tech, a good tech career, good tech effort can look like. I want people to have different role models when it comes to tech “heroes,” let's call them. Because I think, increasingly, we're seeing how the folks who we thought were the tech heroes were not. I think that for folks who are early in their career, I'd like for it to offer something different that resonates.

I think for me now, one of the things that I really value about it is that it's a way for me to spend time with younger people. Because in my job, in my life, I'm with old people now, so it's really exciting and often confusing and sometimes frustrating to hang out with younger people.

**Khasir:**  
\[*laughs*] 

**Gabe:**  
But I think it's really healthy for me. And also, on those occasions when I am able to share opportunities or advice or mentorship, it's really rewarding for me to be able to do that.

**Khasir:**  
I think there's a lot of energy in my generation and the younger generation. There's also a disappointment in the world that's turned out. If we can build a better world, that's all we want.

Well said.
