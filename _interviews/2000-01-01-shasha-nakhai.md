You might know Shasha Nakhai for directing *Scarborough* or producing *The Sandbox*. She’s very friendly ... ?

---

{: style="max-width: 26rem"}
> Why only use black-and-white ink when you have the whole colour palette to use?
> {: .large}

{% floating title="**connecting through technologists for democracy**" %}

**Khasir:**   
Hi Shasha.

**Shasha:**  
Hello!

**Khasir:**   
My first question for you is, we've done a lot of collaborations around [Technologists for Democracy](https://techfordemocracy.ca/), and that's also how we got to know each other. What drew you in to TfD in the first place and what keeps you coming back?

**Shasha:**  
I first found out about Civic Tech Toronto because I was previously working on a project with some of the people who worked on organizing [Block Sidewalk](https://www.policyalternatives.ca/news-research/blocksidewalk-the-last-progressive-nimby-effort/). I was in touch with them and I saw an article in the CBC about a data centre coming to Etobicoke. I was just concerned and also thinking it was important that people were vigilant about that type of thing.

So I asked the organizer, Thorben, if there was anyone doing work on data centres, or just more generally like a tech watchdog. He pointed me in the direction of TfD. And I went on the website and all of the things aligned with what my interests were at the time.

And then, I think the first event I went to was—you called me and then told me about it. It was about the victory party at Paupers Pub for the pilot cancellation.

**Khasir:**  
I guess that was around sometime late last year. That was a joint meetup between the [Tech Workers Coalition](https://techworkerscoalition.org/canada/) and TfD. And also coincided with the end of Magna pilot.

**Shasha:**  
Yeah, I just remember everyone being very welcoming and having some really good in-depth conversations about the state of the world. And it was something I was looking for. I didn't realize it, but on a personal level, I did a lot of activist-y stuff growing up in university, and shortly after. But over time, it evolved that my work became my activism, and it became the way to shape conversations, narratives, and to build the world in the way that you want to see them built.

But recently, it's just felt like it’s not enough because film projects can take five years to make, and I feel like so much of what's happening right now with technology is so urgent. So I think I needed something more immediate.

**Khasir:**   
You talked about your film being your activism. And most recently, you produced *[The Sandbox](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt27557529/)*, which we will be watching at [Film Club](https://luma.com/techfordemocracy) this Friday![^1] It's a film about how surveillance targets the margins of society and expands from there. I thought the culminating scene, where the migrants are trying to escape and are rescued by the speed boat, was a really powerful moment in the film.

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**filmmaking**" %}

One thing that struck me is how surveillance seems to be growing on a global scale, where you point at examples in different locations around the world. What are some of the challenges you face in producing a film like that? And with a message that's spread across the world like that? I know you've mentioned it was multiple stories, and you tried to find a narrative that combined them all.

**Shasha:**  
I'm not gonna go into too much detail on how that film ended up to be what it is, but a big part of the process of making that film was that it was also made by a first-time filmmaker. Someone who had worked in human rights, but it was their first film that they were making. So, a lot of the development process was shaped by that. She had been following an investigative journalist who was working in these spaces, and that's how a lot of the access to these spaces came about.

We really wanted it to be a global project because, first of all, a lot of what was happening on the periphery is still happening in darkness and by design. We wanted to make sure it was global, so people could connect the dots more globally and understand how something happening in a refugee camp in the Kenya-Somalia border can have real-world implications for us over here. How interconnected really everything is. That was the primary purpose between that being very global and choosing to focus on the things that we did. And, of course, a lot of it was just the logistics of production and the process of documentary and shaping and finding a story.

**Khasir:**  
Well, I really love the movie, so I’m glad it worked out.

**Shasha:**  
Thank you. 

**Khasir:**  
Something I've noticed in the films that you've directed and produced is, there’s an underlying theme of asking, “What does it mean to be human? What does it mean to be part of a society?” So, what are some of the messages that you're hoping to impart on your audience when you're making these films?

**Shasha:**  
It’s very interesting, I've only recently actually started reflecting on the common thread between all of my work, because it's just been whatever interests me and my partner. We've recently had to do pitch meetings where we're supposed to talk about the types of films we like to make. We've had to package ourselves more. And I’m realizing a lot of our films have to do with very big industries, very big systems, and how they show up on a micro/individual level and also on a collective level.

So, I think that's kind of a common thread. Even *The Sandbox*. It's the intersection of late stage capitalism, techno-solutionism and the colonial project renewed. It’s the intersection of all these different forces and the COVID pandemic at one point as well. We try to show how all of these big, big forces interplay and show up in people's lives.

**Khasir:**  
I think that's an important message. So many people are just living their lives. It's very easy to lose the forest for the trees and just focus on your own thing, and not realize there are wider forces at play and shaping our lives that you have no direct control over.

So, you make a lot of films—you’ve transitioned from being a journalist into a filmmaker. We were talking a bit about that on our walk here to Grange Park. Was that something that you knew you always wanted to go into, moving into documentaries and cataloguing life? Or was that something that you stumbled upon?

**Shasha:**  
I've always been interested in human rights and politics and socio-political issues. But I think what made me really want to do journalism was when the Iraq War was happening. I just thought it was fascinating how, when you shine a light on something and cover it in the news, it can have a tremendous impact.

That's what I was really interested in, and after going to journalism school, I began to realize over time that holding people captive in the cinema for an hour and a half has a much deeper effect than a 30-second news bite. Because I was really interested in the impact part of it, and I just love cinema. Why only use black-and-white ink when you have the whole colour palette to use? That's what I really liked about documentary filmmaking in particular.

**Khasir:**  
Something that I've been also reflecting on, is how art comments on society and can also shape society, while at the same time, showing a side of yourself, as the creator, to society. It's a multifaceted kind of thing, where you have a message you want to impart, and you can't help but also imbue parts of yourself into it and reflect the society that you're a part of.

Is that something that you see in the pieces that you make? That there's a little part of yourself that you put into it?

**Shasha:**  
For sure, I would say so. What guides the things I make is, really, I just make what I want to see. A lot of what I wanted to see has also just been things that haven't been made before, so it very much informs what the next projects I have upcoming are. That's my guiding force—just make what I would want to see, or what I would have wanted to see growing up.

I mean, I have a very personal project I'm in development on, so that's even more putting yourself out there, which is very uncomfortable. But it's definitely part of the process of filmmaking. It's like a dialogue and also like a therapy. The part of making films is also a grappling with the big questions. So, it's kind of a weird therapy in some regards.

{% endfloating %}

{% floating title="**overlapping film and tech**" %}

**Khasir:**  
Coming back to the idea of Civic Tech—I think a lot of people forget about the civic side of civic tech, and they think only of the tech side. And personally, I consider what you are doing to be civic tech.

**Shasha:**  
Which is hilarious, because I'm not a tech person. I'm probably the least technologist at Technologist for Democracy. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
That's okay! In terms of the civic side, you're shining a lens on what it means to participate in society. That's civics, right? In terms of filmmaking, I think that there is an important overlap between the civic side of things.

I know you may not consider yourself as much of a part of civic tech, and you’ve joked about it before—do you think that what you do ties in with the things that civic tech are doing? Or do you see that maybe this is a different thing?

**Shasha:**  
I definitely see a synergy there. And these days, media and tech are all merging to almost become the same thing. So I feel, if you want to do anything or have any impact in the world, you need to be in the technology sphere. It just so happens that the most recent projects I'm working on are very tech-focused. We have worked on tech-focused ones in the past. It's also just the times we live in.

In terms of my role, I think I try to humble myself every time I come to Civic Tech Toronto where I recognize that there are so much smarter people here who know very specific things. But that's what I like about coming to Civic Tech. I like being in an environment where I don't know everything, and learning new things. I like being led by people who are experts, too, in the thing that we're trying to tackle. People bring different expertise and different skillsets.

And it's an offset for the big, scary technocrats out there. One can feel very powerless on a day-to-day basis. So it's nice to see people using technology to build things that better, and to build the world that they want to see.

In film and media, a lot of what we do—especially when it comes to the tech environment right now—is complain about it, or write about it, or make a film about it. But it's nice to see people doing things. While I know that that's not my expertise, I enjoy being amongst people who are doing that, and I can contribute in my own small ways to the efforts that people are working on.

**Khasir:**  
I'm very grateful that you started the Film Club.[^1]

**Shasha:**  
Thank you!

**Khasir:**  
I think that’s a really good example of how the film world and the tech world can come together.

**Shasha:**  
For sure. I think that there really is a power in film and media, and it's not always fully harnessed. It's nice to a) support these films, but also b) for them to support the work that TfD is doing, by bringing new membership or interest. Because so many times people watch a movie, and they ask what to do about it? And it's nice to point people in the right direction. 

**Khasir:**  
The line from inspiration to direct action. 

**Shasha:**  
Yeah. Now's not really the time—I mean, we can ponder these big philosophical questions, but we need to move because other people are moving faster than us. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
\[laughs] Do you think that there are a lot of filmmakers out there that are exploring the more technical side of things? You just mentioned, for example, that a lot of people will complain or make a film about it. Do you see people trying to bridge the divide between both sides? Or is that not as common a thing?

**Shasha:**  
I think there has been a siloing in the past. That is what I’m trying to do too with Film Club, is have more of an overlap. I’m seeing a growing number of people tackling tech stories. Before, it was just one or two people you could name who specialize in those spheres, but I think filmmakers are realizing that to tell a story about the world we live in today is to tell a tech story.

For example, Javier, Hannah and Mark, who are working on a facial recognition project, are recognizing their knowledge gaps as filmmakers. So they're trying to partner with people who have deeper expertise or who can help guide them. So yeah, I'm seeing the overlap growing. I think it's very important.

**Khasir:**  
Why do you think now is the time that people have decided to try to bridge the gap as opposed to five years ago? 

**Shasha:**  
I'm not sure, but I think it probably has something to do with the dying of traditional journalism. The conglomerates conglomerating, becoming bigger and bigger. Media conglomerates just getting eaten up left, right, and centre. Local journalism, independent journalism, suffering. And because there's also immense power behind tech and the tech narrative.

So I think people understand that a big part of fighting against that is fighting the narratives. That probably has something to do with the increase. Even just to be a journalist and write about something mundane today also requires you to have cybersecurity in place, and all of these other things, so it's not really avoidable anymore.

**Khasir:**  
Is that because there's a greater risk of being hacked?

**Shasha:**  
There's a much greater risk of being targeted, most especially if you're speaking truth to power. So yes, journalists are now having more overlap with tech and security folks than they might have had in the past.

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**picking a side**" %}

**Khasir:**  
Do you think journalists should pick a side, or do you think journalists should try to stay as neutral as possible in terms of reporting things?

**Shasha:**  
That's an interesting question. It's a very long thing we can discuss for hours! I think there is a role for neutral journalism. Unfortunately, when we're talking about the big tech divide, there's just facts that would make them pick a side, if you know what I mean. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
\[laughs] I think I know what you mean!

**Shasha:**  
It's hard to be subjective about some things. Like total annihilation of the human race! I think you can pick a side. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
Well… \[laughs] I think you're on to something here.

There are a lot of examples of—I was reading an [article today about techno-solutionism](https://vsanchezgomez.substack.com/p/technosolutionism-part1). Someone, who reached out to me on LinkedIn and seemed very interesting actually, had written an article about it. He had an interesting metaphor talking about how a lot of tech companies and AI companies these days are selling products to solve problems that their other products created.

**Shasha:**  
\[laughs] The endless scam.

**Khasir:**  
The endless scam. It's like a cycle, like a serpent eating its tail. Meta, for example, trying to sell this AI companion that will keep people company because people are lonely. And why are they lonely? People who use social media a lot tend to be lonelier. And why is that? Because they're online and not socializing in person.

He had a very good metaphor. Imagine, 200 years ago, you’re a child, and you're working in the mines. Your hands are tired and your body's tired. So the mine owner sells you this cream that heals you. You feel better and ready to go back in the mines. And you would say, yes, this cream is helpful. We should have more of it. \[laughs] That's what the tech companies are doing.

**Shasha:**  
Yeah, I think also one of the interesting things we found when we were working on The Sandbox was, we thought we would find this hyper self-aware, hyper-secretive, hyper-connected international network of extreme conspiracy. But, to be honest, a lot of it was people who didn't even know how things worked, and they just bought something because it was pitched to them and told it would work.

And I think so much of this scamming and selling the empty box is happening because we have a lot of boomers, and older decision-makers who aren't necessarily technologically literate. They just keep buying these things. I think people becoming more technologically literate is very important.

**Khasir:**  
It sounds to me that we're going in this direction because some people are leading the pack and others are not fully informed of the risks and the trade-offs. It's easy to attribute malice to this kind of thing when it's actually just whatever is easiest for most people. 

**Shasha:**  
There is some deep malice there, but the majority of it is just bumbling. \[laughs]

**Khasir:**  
\[laughs] 

{% endfloating %}
{% floating title="**the meaning of civic tech toronto**" %}

It’s getting dark, and I’m worried it might rain. So I’ll wrap it up with one more question, one that I’m asking to everyone that I'm talking to. As someone who attends meetups at Civic Tech Toronto, is a member of TfD, and even suggested [Dan Bekerman](https://civictech.ca/events/555/) as a speaker—what does Civic Tech Toronto mean to you, personally?

**Shasha:**  
For me, it's a place to find community. Find like-minded people interested in the same things. And a place to practice a different modality too. To do something completely outside of my day-to-day and learn something new.
