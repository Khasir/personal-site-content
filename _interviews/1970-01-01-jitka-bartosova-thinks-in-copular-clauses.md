For the last post of 2026, I’m sharing a conversation I had in October with Jitka,[^1] a linguist and independent researcher from the Czech Republic. We’ve become friends through a shared sense that we can make the world a better place, and from presenting similar topics at the Fabric unconference last year (which I almost didn’t attend due to a bike accident, but that’s a story for another time).

---

{% floating title="**connecting in canada**" %}

**Khasir:**  
I wanted to start with how we met, which was last year in this room. We were at the [Fabric unconference](https://ma.to/event/fabric-11-jul-2025) hosted by [1RG](https://1rg.space/). The unconference was basically where people came together and discussed ideas, and also created the schedule for discussing how to use technology to improve our lives. How did you decide that a conference where the people decide what the topics are was something that you wanted to attend?

**Jitka:**  
I don't think I was this thorough in making a decision that way! It was more, at that time, I was shopping around and seeing what kinds of events were out there. I also had a recent experience around that time from [Toronto Tech Week](https://www.torontotechweek.com/), where I was specifically at this other event, and I felt like that was not very close to my values. And when I was reading about Fabric, that seemed a lot more aligned. So I didn't actually even think too much about the format. But I was thinking, maybe I can meet some people who think alike.

**Khasir:**  
And we met! Was there anything else that stood out to you at the conference?

**Jitka:**  
It just felt like a very comfortable place to be in, and people were very friendly and open. I actually really like the space too, 1RG. Also because I haven't been here for that long. In general, I think it was the first time when I felt like I was among people who I want to be around. \[*laughs*] I think that was an event that has changed a lot of my experience in Canada, by meeting people who think a little differently than the mainstream and care about humans. \[*laughs*] Or more like, who are more critical around some stuff, and have more humanity-oriented values. That was nice.

So yes, I have met also other people here. It was amazing to meet you. And some of these people I stayed in contact with, like [Kexin](https://seagullcounting.club/), and I met [Bianca](https://biancawylie.com/) here for the first time, actually. I do follow her everywhere. \[*laughs*]

**Khasir:**  
\[*laughs*] You’re a big fan. 

**Jitka:**  
I really like her thinking about stuff and talking at events. I like to listen to her.

**Khasir:**  
Yeah, she's great. You mentioned, you hadn't been here for very long. Did you mean in Toronto, or in Canada?

**Jitka:**  
I was here between 2013 and 2017 doing my PhD, but it was in Hamilton, not in Toronto. I was coming to Toronto, but I was very busy doing the PhD, and so I wasn't really connected to people in Toronto, or events, or even civic society. I didn't know much about it. At that point, I wasn't even really following politics or anything like that—just doing my PhD with a plan to go home. During this time, I met my partner here. And then I already decided to move back to my country, which is the Czech Republic, in 2017.

But then things changed. We were in the Czech Republic, and we had to come back in 2024. That year, I was still living in Hamilton, but we knew we wanted to move to Toronto because we had some friends here, and knew that there were going to be a lot more opportunities. I was mostly really looking for a job, but also for community for sure. 

So I wasn't in Canada for long, and at that time I wasn't even in Toronto, but I wanted to see what's here, and what kind of people are here, and how I can even do some work. I was also very passionate about some things at that time, and I felt a lot of self-righteousness in some topics. I think that the topic that I brought here to 1RG was accountability, which is still a big topic for me. How do we keep more powerful parties accountable for doing things that are not okay?

**Khasir:**  
I can think of a few examples right now. [Bike lanes](https://thewalrus.ca/doug-ford-is-spending-a-lot-of-money-ripping-out-bike-lanes/), for example. Or even [Waymo coming to Canada](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/waymo-autonomous-driving-robotaxi-toronto-2028-9.7383437), which we learned this morning.

{% floating title="**copular clauses are cool**" %}

I wanted to jump back a bit. You mentioned doing your PhD in Hamilton. Can you remind me again what it was about? I remember something about Czech pronouns.

**Jitka:**  
The PhD was in cognitive science of language. Part of it was my [thesis](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/items/ce3fd537-f7c2-4910-a7ce-40f30bb9d705), which was the biggest chunk, of course, and that was about a specific pronoun. But it wasn't even really about just the Czech language. I was looking at similarly-behaving particles in different languages as well. It was specifically in the context of something called [copular clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copula_\(linguistics\)), which are sentences where you have usually two nouns (but you can have something else) connected with the verb to be.

The reason why I was looking at these specific pronouns was actually, really, semantics. I was working with the syntactic theory of [generative grammarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar). [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky). While he had his whole syntax figured out, it was not always mapping directly to semantics. Specifically this pronoun, this particle, didn’t seem to be easily explained by the theory of syntax. Its meaning, its mapping. It behaves weirdly in different languages and different contexts. I was trying to bring more understanding into how syntax maps on semantics.

**Khasir:**  
Okay. Why? \[*laughs*]

**Jitka:**  
\[*laughs*] Because, basically, when I was applying for the PhD, I knew I was going to be working with this specific advisor. I had to choose one of her topics for SSHRC funding, and this was the one that made the most sense. It was also connected to linguists from the University of Toronto. So from the very beginning, I started working on the topic of copular clauses, which was the general topic. Later on, it started to emerge as something potentially interesting within that project. So it wasn't really me coming up with the topic completely, it was more that I knew I wanted to work on this because I also like the other professors from here. I just found a little area in that.

But to me, the whole PhD wasn't really just about writing this thesis. That definitely changed. \[*laughs*] It was fun. I loved the actual PhD studies because I learned so much in a lot of other areas. I like how PhDs are done here. I don't know if it's normal, but you basically study your Masters while you study your PhD, so I had all the courses for a Master's degree here that taught me a lot of new, cool stuff actually. So it was very enriching. 

**Khasir:**  
I actually don't know very much about PhDs, so I don't know if it's normal or not here.

**Jitka:**  
I think if you are not an international student, you would probably do your Masters and then do your PhD and that is going to be mostly focused on the thesis. But as I was doing my Masters in the Czech Republic, I came here and had to have the full curriculum done before my PhD. I guess it made it different for me. Or it happens to people who are maybe transferring fields or something like that.

{% floating title="**activism and media attention**" %}

**Khasir:**  
Going back to the idea of accountability—we do a lot of collaboration at [Technologists for Democracy](https://techfordemocracy.ca/), and one of the first things that we worked on together was the Cineplex Digital Media camera billboards that were [spying on people and doing facial detection](https://toronto.citynews.ca/2025/12/08/privacy-commissioner-investigating-digital-ads-that-use-facial-detection-near-union-station/).[^2] The research and the strategy that you helped with was very valuable. I'm very glad that you were there to help out. Why did you decide to trust me, and trust the campaign, and trust that something would happen?

**Jitka:**  
I think that a really large selling point to join Technologists for Democracy was your previous success, which was the [Magna International case](https://www.thestar.com/news/gta/you-never-know-why-companies-do-things-magna-ends-its-driverless-vehicle-test-early-in/article_ed0a0f2b-0fb0-425d-8509-8fee5d2237fa.html) we were looking into. And also, really early I just trusted in your leadership and instincts a lot. First of all, it felt really nice and natural to be in this group. I wanted to put some energy into something that mattered, specifically the campaign. And also, I think it touched me in general, the Cineplex billboards. Because I felt like it was really such an obvious example of a company just wanting to make money anywhere in any ways, potentially through—we still don't know, but—targeted marketing to faces. It felt dystopian to me. I felt strongly about it, but just feeling strongly about it wasn't enough. It was really the group that made it feel very natural to work on something with you.

**Khasir:**  
Did you have any expectations for how you thought this would go, or did you just have—free flowing, anything happens…?

**Jitka:**  
It was more like anything happens, happens. I didn't know. But also, I think the important part is that I'm learning a lot. I feel like I have learned through this case, through even your retrospective of the Magna case before. During this time, I've learned a lot, and I usually do things this way. I don't really have specific expectations of what's going to happen, but I know I'm going to learn a lot during the process. And I did.

**Khasir:**  
Was there anything that surprised you in the process? Or you were so open that, if you have no expectations, nothing can surprise you?

**Jitka:**  
I think I was open. There were definitely small surprises. The fact that somehow, somebody from our group was connected to the CTO of Cineplex at that time. That was very surprising. \[*laughs*] I don't think anybody expected that.

I did find it surprising—and this is something I'm still trying to understand and learn here—how the whole media attention happens. It was really fast and strong here at one point, and I didn't expect that. I was a little startled by that too, because I feel like it works differently than where I'm from.

**Khasir:**  
How does it work differently?

**Jitka:**  
I don't think I was doing the same stuff over there... It's very hard for me to compare, but I do think one thing, and that is: if something like this showed up in the Czech Republic, then it would probably get into the purely most left-wing anarchist media. I don't know if it would ever find its way into a more global media.

**Khasir:**    
Really?

**Jitka:**  
Yeah, but also at the same time, this same stuff would probably not happen due to GDPR. Also, I have a strong suspicion—no, it's not a suspicion—that civic society doesn't work as well over there. There are not that many people who would be doing similar jobs. I think also, this is probably very specific to Toronto. I don't actually know if this would work similarly, if you did something like that in Calgary.

**Khasir:**  
That's interesting. As someone who's grown up in Canada, I imagine Europe to be more progressive in general. But you saying that only really left-wing media would pick it up surprises me.

**Jitka:**  
It depends definitely on where you are within Europe, also. When I'm trying to compare contexts, I'm learning a little bit more about the Czech Republic and in many ways, it is kind of stuck, a little more conservative, and a little more driven by businesses. While there are other countries where it's not as bad.

For instance, I was just in the Czech Republic over the summer, and I saw something like Magna’s little robot-delivery things. I'm pretty sure nobody’s picking that up, writing about it, looking into this, or fighting this in any ways.

**Khasir:**  
I guess it's a different culture. 

**Jitka:**  
Yeah, it is.

**Khasir:**  
Another thing that you're working on with TfD is a privacy checklist. Could you tell our readers about this?

**Jitka:**  
The whole idea started differently, but it has led us, the group, working on this together to explain the Canadian privacy landscape or privacy rights to people who might not be aware of that. I noticed that people are not really as aware of the legislation that currently exists. Also, it is a little complicated over here because you have some provincial stuff, you have some federal stuff, and you have some private companies and public institutions. Different rules apply, so it's a little complicated landscape. When we started finding out how complicated it is, we realized that most people definitely don't know about this, and probably don't know about what their rights really are. It's hard to fight for something when you don't know what your rights are. That was the basic idea. We wanted to create a tool that would make this simplified, and people could understand better what they could expect from companies and what companies shouldn't be doing with their data.

Right now, we have a very long version of this list that was created through a few rounds of feedback, AI-assisted. We're thinking about turning it into something more accessible. That was the original idea, to make it accessible, to maybe even make it into something more gamified. We're going to talk about this later today. \[*laughs*]

The idea is to empower. I think about it more from the perspective of, for instance, how unions are trying to make employees aware of their rights. I'm thinking about it this way for privacy. Making people understand that they can fight for their rights and that they have them. Even though there’s still not much enforcement in Canada. The rights are there, it's written there, the institution is here still.

**Khasir:**  
What do you hope the impact would be of this checklist?

**Jitka:**  
I would really like if it somehow got to people who were not aware, helped to bring awareness, and then empowered them to act. And I'm not talking about [Bill C-36](https://www.michaelgeist.ca/2026/06/billc36/) \[*laughs*] but in an ideal case later on, there would be legislation in the future that would enforce this stuff more. It would be nice to spread this so that the public is more aware about what their rights are. I don't know how to do that. I've never done that. \[*laughs*] I would like to figure that out.

**Khasir:**  
Right, and the power of a community is, we can figure it out together.

**Jitka:**  
Yes, exactly.

{% floating title="**the meaning of civic tech toronto**" %}

Alright, last question. This is a question that I've been asking everyone that I'm interviewing: what does Civic Tech Toronto mean to you?

**Jitka:**  
It's a community of very welcoming people of diverse experiences and interests that, in most cases, really have civics in its core. To me, it provides safety with regularity. You can always rely on there being weekly meetings, and you can always go and meet people that you know. I have never heard of anything similar anywhere else. It's really great. I love the openness. I love how much effort has been put into making this a really inclusive space that welcomes newcomers, that helps out people, that creates a community, and brings a lot of new knowledge and new, interesting stuff.

I totally admire all the organizers that put a lot of effort into this. I believe it has already led to so many really cool things, so much good organizing. For me now it feels a little like home, my community, people I feel close with and share values. I'm not saying that everybody within the community is always like that, but with a close circle like Technologists for Democracy, I think we and a lot of other people are like home.

I don't always come but I'm always excited to go. Sometimes, I'm really tired and don't want to go because it takes a long time to get there \[*laughs*] but I always feel better after these meetings. It's very energizing.

**Khasir:**  
Yeah, it is very energizing. There's an energy that it generates by bringing that many people together to learn and work on things.

**Jitka:**  
The regularity is just amazing. The fact that this has kept on like that. You feel like, maybe I'm going to miss out on a month or two months, but then you can come back and continue with this. It feels really safe.
