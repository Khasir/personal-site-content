[Civic Tech Toronto](https://civictech.ca/) (CTTO) as an organization is structured around two main things: speakers and projects. That said, speakers bring with them their own projects\* to present. So really, the essence of Civic Tech Toronto can be boiled down to civic tech projects.

\*When I talk about lowercase civic tech projects, I mean broadly anything that’s 1. been worked on (project) and 2. serves the public (civic) in a technical way (tech).[^1] A [website to track live transit vehicle locations](https://busferret.ca/) is a civic tech project. [Research on AI and human behaviour](https://www.bi.team/publications/ai-and-human-behaviour/) is a civic tech project. A [Facebook group for people to share housing opportunities](https://www.facebook.com/groups/torontohomezone/) is a civic tech project. Speakers for all of these projects have presented at Civic Tech Toronto.[^2]

Inspired by Wrye’s [post on cathedrals and parlours](https://wryemusings.com/Cathedral%20vs.%20Parlor.html) in the context of the Morrowind modding community, and as a complement to Ilya and patcon’s [presentation on organizing for civic tech](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1vhZub7vI9BPBqrSgighLvyofWG6eexpF/edit?usp=sharing&ouid=111470149770930773202&rtpof=true&sd=true),[^3] I posit that there are three fundamental philosophies that motivate people to work on projects in the broader civic tech community:

1. Projects as *cathedrals*
2. Projects as *parlours*
3. Projects as *mansions*

None of these philosophies are “good” or “bad” in and of themselves, but some philosophies fit with certain goals better than others. I’ll briefly cover these three philosophies, followed by a more in-depth exploration of why Civic Tech Toronto has encouraged cathedrals over other types of projects.

## projects as cathedrals

In this philosophy, projects are a joint effort that builds upon each other’s contributions. This paragraph by Wrye sums it up nicely:

> Individually, our contributions may be small – and may not be worth doing for themselves. But by each person contributing something, we construct something larger and more worthwhile than any of us could do on our own. Under this view, creations are contributions – and may not be taken back. (Just as in building a Cathedral, it would not be allowed for a person to contribute a stained glass window and then later take it back.)

Cathedrals prioritize community collaboration at the cost of singular ownership. These projects don’t depend on one single person to keep going, but instead build upon and are dependent on the work of many volunteers. As a result, cathedrals are often less hierarchical than your typical organization.

Grassroots community groups and open source projects tend to fall into this category. [Civic Dashboard](https://civicdashboard.ca/),[^4] [Chorus](https://github.com/choruslabs/chorus) and [Bikespace](https://bikespace.ca/) are three projects coming out of Civic Tech Toronto that I would say embody this philosophy. In the wild, we can look to [DeFlock](https://deflock.org/), [Toronto Games Week](https://torontogamesweek.com/index.html) and the [Tech Workers Coalition](https://techworkerscoalition.org/) as civic tech cathedrals.

## projects as parlours

In this philosophy, projects are personal or exclusive efforts that others are allowed to enjoy at the creator’s discretion. Again, Wrye puts it well:

> The \[creator] invites others into the parlor to appreciate and enjoy the work of art – but may at any time close the parlor door and ask their guests to leave. And of course, the \[creator] may be very selective about who they invite into their parlor. Under this view, our creations are never contributions; rather we continue to own and control them – \[takedowns] are normal and accepted.

Parlours prioritize ownership and control by their creators at the cost of publicly-collaborative effort. This way, it’s easier to maintain focus on core principles or features while still serving public needs. However, these parlours can be taken down or made unavailable at any time, depending on the creator’s level of interest or funding.

Personal projects typically fall into this category, and sometimes nonprofits as well. [Democracy Distributed](https://democracydistributed.com/), [OpenPolicy.me](https://openpolicy.me/) and [TOBus.ca](http://tobus.ca) are three parlours coming out of Civic Tech Toronto. Outside of Civic Tech Toronto, there’s [OpenParliament.ca](http://openparliament.ca), [itsneerby](https://www.itsneerby.ca/) and countless transit apps like this [TTC Delay Analysis](https://ttcdelay.kbains.com/) app.[^5]

## projects as mansions

In this philosophy, projects are exclusive efforts prioritizing financial stability and control by their creators. Members of the public do not necessarily interact directly with mansions, but can still feel their effects. Mansions often start out as parlours, but sometimes as cathedrals too.

Nonprofits, educational institutions and for-profit corporations tend to fall into this category. On the nonprofit and educational side, we have the [Mozilla Foundation](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/), and the programs and research hosted and published by the University of Toronto’s [School of Cities](https://schoolofcities.utoronto.ca/)[^6] as examples. On the corporate side, we have [Miovision](https://miovision.com/),[^7] [Palantir](https://www.palantir.com/), [YouGov](https://yougov.com/) and Google’s [Sidewalk Labs](https://web.archive.org/web/20200508015227/https://www.sidewalklabs.com/)[^8] all being mansions. [DemocracyXChange](https://www.democracyxchange.org/) (originally [Democracy Kit](https://opendemocracy.ca/))[^9] and [Code for Canada](https://codefor.ca/) are two examples of mansions that have arisen from Civic Tech Toronto.

## civic tech toronto encourages cathedrals

> Civic Tech Toronto’s main output isn’t websites, it’s organizers.
> 
> — [patcon](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1vhZub7vI9BPBqrSgighLvyofWG6eexpF/edit?slide=id.p12#slide=id.p12)
> {: .attribution}

Civic Tech Toronto, as a purely volunteer-run organization, operates with very little funding. The largest expense of CTTO itself is the samosas provided to attendees for free every week. It otherwise lacks any financial ambitions, which trickles down as a philosophy passed onto most projects it is host to. As such, mansions tend not to operate or grow out of CTTO, even as they come to present their ideas. Meanwhile, parlours and cathedrals occupy the space as they both share a lack of dependency on funding. 

Beyond that, I believe that a lack of financial motivation is not reason enough for cathedrals to flourish here. What I think makes CTTO better than other *typical techosphere* makerspaces at incubating cathedrals are three essential ingredients:

1. A general lack of financial motivation or continual reliance on funding,
2. An emphasis on improving public life, and
3. Weekly meetups for learning and collaboration.

Subtract any of these, and you get spaces that tend to encourage parlours or mansions over cathedrals by default.

## but really, why cathedrals?

> People are 99% of civic tech.
> 
> — [Grace O’Hara](https://blog.codeforaustralia.org/to-the-women-of-civic-tech-f5961fd1eda2)
> {: .attribution}

You might still be asking, why does CTTO push for cathedrals over other forms of organization? Well, there are a few reasons:

- *Civic tech is a joint effort:* By its very nature, working on civic issues builds upon the knowledge, work and effort of trailblazers and others who have contributed in this space. 
- *Cathedrals celebrate community:* By prioritizing collaboration, cathedrals work best when people form communities of practice and [work together](https://www.citizenshandbook.org/working_collectively.pdf).
- *Cathedrals prevent reinventing the wheel:* Instead of going off to do something new on your own, or distinguishing yourself with a unique selling proposition, you can join a cathedral and improve on the work of countless people who have come before you.
- *Cathedrals have lasting power:* There’s a comfort in belonging to a community that will have a life beyond the time of your own involvement, knowing that you can safely leave when you need to, and return when you’re ready.

You could honestly encourage mansions for the same reasons as the above, except for one large caveat I’ve already mentioned—Civic Tech Toronto operates without depending on money. In which case I will add:

- *Cathedrals don’t need much money to operate.*

## okay, so shouldn’t you just get funding then?

> For me civic tech is not about getting things done fast, because getting things done fast is not an inclusive way to do things …
> 
> — [Emily Macrae](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.06651#page=19)
> {: .attribution}

Well, you certainly could. But money is not, by default, a silver bullet to all your problems. In his presentation, Ilya [compares money to gravity](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1vhZub7vI9BPBqrSgighLvyofWG6eexpF/edit?slide=id.p5#slide=id.p5) that ties down most organizations—if you structure an organization around requiring money to operate, it becomes very easy to become dependent on it. Many organizations are [designed to look good](https://employerbranding.news/the-performative-trap-of-workplace-authenticity/) [instead of doing good](https://www.bridgespan.org/insights/nonprofit-organizational-effectiveness/what-is-impact-measurement-and-how-can-organizations-use-it). Financial dependency only exacerbates that risk, as you need to then prove to the world that you are effective by doing things with high visibility and not necessarily high impact. But by embracing a lack of financial dependence, you don’t have to subject yourself to the limitations of a traditional organization.

If you do want money, you should ask yourself what you would do with it. A few of us have wrestled with this question with regards to [Technologists for Democracy](https://techfordemocracy.ca/) (T*f* D) which I am a co-organizer of. Most people will tell you to seek funding in order to run a volunteer organization properly. And that’s fair, sometimes you need to pay someone to do the boring administrative work or be available at a moment’s notice and respond to urgent requests. But it’s hard to fire someone once you start paying a salary—suddenly the organization becomes responsible for their livelihood so they can afford their rent or support a family. That’s a lot of pressure to keep the money coming in, and fundraising isn’t fun. 

Before going for money, you should keep these things in mind:

- *Lack of financial pressure incentivizes experimentation:* No money means a safe space to “fail,” financially speaking, as there are no quarterly targets or KPIs that need to be reached. 
	- Related to this, it becomes easy to lose *spontaneity* (which some would call [freedom](https://youtube.com/shorts/9ejlKXKghmA?si=cCanHk6fQmbiR_fB) or [exercising your free will](https://www.tiktok.com/@biigwaterguy/video/7547032828848999694)) if everything has to be viewed against balancing the books.
- *Money introduces overhead:* Once you’re incorporated and managing money, that introduces a lot of overhead that volunteers are unlikely to take on themselves. This leads to hiring someone to account for the money (or finding good board members).
- *Full-time salaries instate hierarchy:* Once someone is working full-time as a staff member of a primarily volunteer-run organization, it usually leads to more top-down interactions between the staff and volunteers, and (un)intentional separation of information.
- *Civic tech cathedrals are unlikely to sustain funding:* The kind of funding received by most civic tech cathedrals that would “graduate” to become nonprofits, as opposed to for-profit corporations, are likely to be one-time grants. Thus, hiring someone to fundraise full-time tends to become a net zero or even negative contribution to the project as most of their time is spent purely fundraising.
- *Receiving funding means conceding to funders or shareholders:* You know why so many companies are thrusting AI everywhere, to their employees and users? (No, notepad, I do not need Copilot.)
- *Prioritizing profitability clashes with serving the public:* Oftentimes, gaining revenue or raising funds becomes the ultimate goal of an organization, to the detriment of other goals like improving the public commons.

At T*f* D, we’re exploring the idea of a nonprofit. But we’re also currently leaning towards minimal financial dependency, in the same vein as CTTO or [More Neighbours Toronto](https://www.moreneighbours.ca/) which doesn’t currently have any paid staff. The main reason for us forming a nonprofit would not actually be for the funding, but instead the limited legal protections for volunteers as we go after unethical technologies.

With all this in mind, there are certainly upsides to getting money. Being able to properly compensate people for the work and effort they’re putting in. Enabling staff to focus on the mission of an organization. But the way you’d structure an organization for revenue is very different than structuring for one without.
