Feel free to try it now—I can wait.

…

*Nothing* might be your answer. *Your eyelids*. *Undulating shapes and colours* as your eyes adjust to the relative darkness.

<!--{% include figure.html src="/content/images/bacteria.png" alt="Bacteria" align="right" width="80px" %}-->

What if I told you I’ve seen intimately nested rings? Cells and bacteria moving about? The reflection of my own eyeball staring back at me?

Would you believe me? I hardly believe myself.

<hr>

Back in 1917, three young children—Lúcia, Francisco, and Jacinta—in the Portuguese town of Fátima were visited by a vision of the Virgin Mary. She assured them that on the 13th day of each month, She would return, and that on the sixth month, a great miracle would occur.

Months passed. It was now the sixth month, October 1917. In the rain, thousands of people from across the country gathered to bear witness to the rumour of a prophesied miracle. The crowd held its collective breath in rapt anticipation. The Virgin Mary appeared to the three children at noon to assure them of the impending miracle.

{% include figure.html src="/content/images/crowd-fatima.png" alt="The crowd at Fátima, Oct 13, 1917" caption='The crowd at Fátima on October 13, 1917.[^1]' %}

Then suddenly—

> The hour approaches, and behold, as if by magic, the rain stops, the sun breaks through the dense, dark clouds and reveals itself with its luminous rays, which quickly take on the colours of yellow, crimson, and green, turning the objects that were under its influence into similar colours; and then loses its brightness and colors—able to be seen with the naked eye without hurting the eyes—and takes on a dizzying rotation, seeming to plunge toward the earth. And while observing these wonders, everyone was found to be exclaiming loudly. This lasted, at most, about five minutes, then returned to its normal state.[^2]

Hundreds testified to the *Milagre do Sol*, as it came to be known, claiming that the clouds parted, the rain stopped, and the sun appeared to change colour and shape, and move rapidly through the air. Mass panic erupted as the sun approached the crowd. People fell to their knees and prayed to God. Others fainted. And then as quickly as it started, it was all over.

Did this actually happen? Our modern understanding of physics wouldn’t permit for the sun or a bright orb to move through our atmosphere as many eyewitnesses claimed it to do. But on the other hand, thousands of people agreed that something miraculous happened on that day in 1917. 

Is it possible to hold *differing truths simultaneously?*

<hr>

Almost 10 years ago, I got a random phone call. I decided to answer it on a whim. I was confronted with an angry man with an Indian accent. He claimed that my daughter trespassed on his property and kicked his dog. Bewildered and confused, I told him that I was too young to have a daughter in the first place, at which point he kept yelling at me. I was much more mild-mannered then, so I had the decency to tell him goodbye before hanging up. Nowadays I would hang up sooner.

I posted this incident to Reddit. It was swiftly deleted by a mod. Apparently, it too closely imitated an old comedy act by the Jerky Boys, who became famous for making prank calls just like the one I got.[^3]

For comedy, I didn’t find it very funny. But did it really happen if *only I can say I was called?*

<hr>

The seventh installation of the critically acclaimed *Civilization* series of strategy games, *Sid Meier’s Civilization VII*, came out in 2025 to mixed reviews. It’s a game about leading a civilization to become the best nation, whether through militaristic, cultural, economic or scientific means. 

The original *Civilization* was released in 1991 for the MS-DOS personal computer, when only about one in five families had a computer at home. I wasn’t even born yet. At the time, the *Computer Gaming World* magazine hailed it as “[more addictive than crack](https://web.archive.org/web/20131203114048/http://www.cgwmuseum.org/galleries/issues/cgw_95.pdf#page=121).”

{% include figure.html src="/content/images/civ-map.webp" caption="Looking at the map in *Sid Meier's Civilization*.[^4]" %}

One of the first things you do in *Civilization* is select a leader for your burgeoning nation. Alexander the Great, Abraham Lincoln and Mao Zedong all coexist together simultaneously. The anachronisms made for more interesting gameplay. 

If you were unlucky enough to face off against Mahatma Gandhi, he’d nuke you in the later stages of the game. This was due to a bug where level of aggression would be stored internally as a value between 1 to 10 in an 8-bit unsigned integer.[^5] Normally, when a nation develops into a democracy, it would subtract 2 from its aggression level. But because India already had the lowest default aggression at level 1, it would instead wrap around for Gandhi, setting him at aggression level 255.

{% include figure.html src="/content/images/nuclear-gandhi-donavarion.jpeg" caption="Fan art featuring Gandhi's love of nukes.[^6]" %}

Gandhi’s penchant for nuclear arms became a popular [inside joke](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ep5meQkcCkg) among players. Firaxis, developers of *Civilization* since the third installment, have enshrined this joke into gameplay by giving Gandhi the highest possible values for nuke production in *Civ V*, and a 70% chance of having a hidden “nuke happy” agenda in *Civ VI*.

Now what if I told you that Gandhi’s behaviour in the original Civ was actually a myth? People Make Games [reported](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ur3SdgkW8W4) almost six years ago that the bug was apocryphal.[^7] Sid Meier himself confirmed that Nuclear Gandhi was an urban legend a year later.

I can’t help but feel a disillusionment that’s fallen over the community. Were we better off *not knowing?*

<hr>

I love Toronto. I’ve found a community here. Sure, it takes 15 years to build an LRT line, and we have a corrupt Premier and rampant homelessness, but Toronto is a great city. There’s so much happening in the city every day, and so many communities to take part in. I found [mine](https://www.thestar.com/opinion/contributors/yes-tech-can-be-a-force-for-good-meet-the-people-hacking-toronto-for-the/article_369f65f2-aee4-44ce-a0b9-f2130dfa389e.html) only after getting burned out. Silver lining.

Speaking of why Toronto is great, the level of crime is dropping, with homicides hitting a [50-year low](https://www.thestar.com/news/crime/torontos-set-to-hit-a-50-year-low-in-homicides-so-why-do-many-people/article_175ae6eb-9d16-4990-ba47-b2ffa72ae316.html). Despite this, people don’t feel safe. In fact, three-quarters of Torontonians believe that crime is [getting worse](https://press.liaisonstrategies.ca/perception-gap-gta-residents-believe-crime-is-rising-even-when-it-isnt/), not better. A quarter of men in Toronto feel unsafe on transit. This number rises to a third of women feeling generally unsafe on the TTC, and nearly two thirds of women after 8pm! Add to this that half of all Torontonians have been intimidated or felt threatened on transit.[^8]

Safety is not merely a series of statistics, but also a feeling. If someone tells you they don’t feel safe in a room full of men, you ought to listen and acknowledge them, even if you personally know every man in that room. 

It’s easy to dismiss people based on the facts. But this is how we get populist leaders elected—because people feel dismissed by everyone else, and those leaders speak to fears and desires in a way that no one else does. What’s important *isn’t always the facts.*

<hr>

<!--{% include figure.html src="/content/images/bacteria2.png" alt="Bacteria" align="left" width="80px" %}-->

I started writing this post as an exercise in reflecting on my innate desire for validation, and my relationship with my (now ex) girlfriend.[^9] She grew to become terrified of sketchy people on the TTC, and I wanted to explore and understand how this could come to be. I have my guesses.

It’s funny how hard it is sometimes to determine the truth. And even searching for the truth implies that there is only one correct, objective truth, when oftentimes there can be multiple, indeterminate truths. Or even forgetting that statistics are [interpretations of reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lies,_damned_lies,_and_statistics). 

*Memento mentiri.* The reminder that facts only tell a partial truth. I’ve fallen victim to this tendency to credit facts more than lived experience. I need to remind myself that I’m only human, and mistakes are the best teachers. And that seeking truth is sometimes a fool’s errand. 

All this to say: *Her truth is just as important.*
